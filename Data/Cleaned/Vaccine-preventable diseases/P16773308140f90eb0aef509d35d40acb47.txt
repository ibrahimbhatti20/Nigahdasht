Executive Summary Pakistan has made impressive progress in immunization coverage over the past 30 years with a 117.6% increase in the proportion of children aged 12–23 months fully immunized between 1990 and 2020. With improvements in the vaccination rates, Pakistan was able to reduce the incidence of two vaccine preventable diseases (VPDs) – diphtheria by 94% and measles by 88% over the same period. Despite successive achievements, disparities remain in terms of coverage by vaccine type, socioeconomic background, and demographics. Immunization remains a high priority for the Government of Pakistan and an important element of progress toward universal health coverage and resilient health systems. The government initiated the National Immunization Support Project (NISP) in 2016 with financing from partners including the World Bank, the United States Agency for International Development, Gavi the Vaccine Alliance, and the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation. NISP is a five-year project, scheduled to close in June 2022, which supports and incentivizes the implementation of the government's Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI). NISP has been instrumental in reducing childhood morbidity and mortality from VPDs. Despite significant efforts by the Government and its partners, the immunization coverage is yet to reach the expected benchmarks. Worldwide, Pakistan is one of the two countries where poliomyelitis is still endemic. With respect to coverage of vaccination for diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis/whooping cough, and measles, Pakistan had the second lowest coverage in the South Asia region in 2020. In terms of incidence of VPDs, Pakistan had the second highest incidence of both diphtheria and polio in South Asia in 2020. In comparison to the countries in Eastern and Southern Asia with similar income levels as Pakistan, immunization coverage is relatively low. There is a need to continue to strengthen Pakistan's EPI to improve the health of its children. Further enhancing health systems through an expansion of vaccination sites, among others, and prioritizing lagging areas to maximize immunization coverage in underperforming provinces and districts, are two important. In addition, ensuring sustainable financing for EPI, fostering public–private partnership, and exploring innovative approaches for improving vaccine uptake and ensuring readiness of the supply side, can help improve immunization coverage. 1 1. Background Pakistan is the fifth most populous country in the world, with a projected population of 232 million in 2021, of whom 51 million are women of childbearing age and 33 million children under five years of age. The gross national income per capita was US$1,410 in 2019. Periodic macroeconomic crises and a low human capital basis have constrained the country's growth prospects. Between fiscal years (FY) 2019 and 2020, annual economic growth, measured using gross domestic product (GDP), averaged around 4.3% in Pakistan, below the South Asian annual average of 6.2%. According to the World Bank Human Capital Index, if no improvements in health and education service delivery take place, a Pakistani child born today can expect to be only 40% as productive as s/he otherwise could be by age 18. Pakistan has witnessed some improvements in health, nutrition, and population (HNP) outcomes over the years; significant challenges however remain, especially given the progress made by countries of comparable wealth. Pakistan was not able to achieve any of the 2015 HNP-related millennium development goals; and, at current trends, is unlikely to meet most of the HNP-related Sustainable Development Goals by 2030. Goal 3 of the SDGs is to: “ensure healthy lives and promote wellbeing for all at all ages”. Two of the targets of this goal are, to end preventable deaths of newborns and children under five years of age, with all countries aiming to reduce neonatal mortality—death within the first 28 days of birth—to no more than 12 per 1,000 live births, and under-five mortality (the probability of a child dying before their fifth birthday) to no more than 25 per 1,000 live births by 2030. Pakistan reduced its neonatal mortality rate from 53.3 per 1,000 live births in 1990 to 41 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019, but this rate was still the second highest in the world. Similarly, the under-five child mortality rate ) declined from 120.4 per 1,000 live births in 1990 to 74 per 1,000 live births in 2019. Childhood immunization against vaccine preventable diseases (VPD) presents a highly cost-effective intervention by delivering significant reductions in morbidity and mortality from inexpensive and standardized interventions. It remains one of the most fundamental competencies of public health programs. The Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) has existed in Pakistan since 1978. In line with international standards, EPI in Pakistan aims to immunize all children against VPDs that include childhood tuberculosis, poliomyelitis, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B, haemophilus influenza type b (Hib) pneumonia and meningitis, measles, and diarrhea due to rotavirus. Immunization is a high priority for the Government of Pakistan and an important part of making progress to universal health coverage and building resilient health systems. The government initiated the National Immunization Support Project (NISP) in 2016 with financing from partners including the World Bank, the United States Agency for International Development, Gavi the Vaccine Alliance, and the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation. NISP is a five-year project, scheduled to close in June 2022, that aims to increase equitable coverage of services for immunization against VPDs, including poliomyelitis, for children aged 0–23 months in Pakistan. The project supports and incentivizes the implementation of the government's EPI program, which has contributed to significantly decreasing childhood morbidity and mortality from VPDs. Despite significant efforts by the government and its partners, Pakistan's immunization indicators have yet to reach the expected benchmarks. Pakistan is one of the two countries in the world, where poliomyelitis is still endemic. Demographic Health Surveys are standard internationally accepted surveys administered in nearly 90 countries across the world to collect information on selected health, nutrition, and population outcomes. The cross-sectional TPVICS survey was carried out on children aged 12–23 months with a sample larger than that used for PDHS 2018. TPVICS covered almost 98,000 children, whereas PDHS 2018 covered 2,463 children of 12–23 months. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics (PBS) was consulted for finalizing the sampling frame of the survey. Data was collected over the period from September 2020, starting with Punjab, and ending in January 2021 with Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP). The survey covered 97,760 children across the country, of whom 53,063 were male and 44,697 female. For the purposes of TPVICS, a fully immunized child is defined as one who has completed all immunizations starting with BCG at birth up to the first dose of measles administered at 9 months of age, as per EPI schedule, except for rotavirus vaccine (see Table 1). Like the PDHS, the TPVICS included background characteristics of the child's household, parents' level of educational attainment, and wealth. A household comprises either one person living alone or a group of people living at the same address, who may or may not be related, with common housekeeping, who either share at least one meal a day or share common living accommodation (that is, a living room or sitting room). .. Parental education level is classified into four categories: none (not attended formal schooling), primary education (1–5 years of formal education), middle (6–8 years of formal education), secondary (9–10 years), and higher (11 years or more). Households are divided into five categories (poorest, poor, middle, rich, and richest) of equal size (that is, quintiles each with 20% of the population) using the number and types of consumer goods they own, ranging from a television to a bicycle or car, and housing characteristics such as source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and flooring materials. Immunization coverage in Pakistan: 1990 to 2020 Over the course of 30 years from 1990 to 2020, Pakistan more than doubled its immunization coverage (an increase of 117.6%). During that period the proportion of fully immunized children aged 12–23 months increased from 35% to 76% (see Figure 1). There was no noteworthy variation by gender in 2020, with 76.6% of female children and 76.2% of male children fully vaccinated. Similarly, there was no rural–urban divide, with 76.2% of children fully vaccinated in rural areas in 2020 compared to 76.7% in urban areas, for Pakistan overall. It is important to note that immunization service delivery in 2020 was hampered due to the outbreak of novel coronavirus disease (COVID-19) and, therefore, the immunization coverage rates were lower than planned. Greater immunization coverage over the period meant that Pakistan was able to reduce the incidence of two VPDs—diphtheria and measles—by 94% and measles by 88% respectively between 1990 and 2020 (see Figures 2 and 3). Diphtheria is a serious infection that causes difficulty in breathing, heart failure, paralysis, and even death especially among children. Measles is a highly contagious disease and can be fatal for ... children under five years of age, the elderly, pregnant women, and those with a weakened immune system. ... Both these diseases can be prevented through vaccination. Pakistan also reduced the incidence of poliomyelitis (a disabling, sometimes fatal disease) between 1990 and 2020 from 777 to 135 cases. Despite this progress, Pakistan is one of the two countries in the world where poliomyelitis is still endemic. According to WHO, eradication of poliomyelitis requires high immunization coverage to block transmission of the virus that is extremely contagious. Several factors explain why some children are still not being vaccinated. Gaps in immunization coverage Although immunization coverage has improved over time, disparities exist in terms of coverage by vaccine type, socioeconomic background, and demographics. There is considerable variation in coverage of each type of vaccine among the provinces. Punjab documented the highest coverage for every vaccine while Balochistan has lagged behind (see Table 2). More importantly, Pakistan's immunization coverage is low compared to other countries in the region. The burden of disease remains high, with the incidence of both diphtheria and polio in Pakistan being the second highest in 2020. More worrying still is that Pakistan is one of only two countries in the world where polio is still prevalent. When compared to countries in Eastern and Southern Asia that have a similar level of wealth, Pakistan's immunization coverage ranks very low. These facts point to the need to strengthen Pakistan's immunization program to improve the health of its children. · Prioritize lagging areas: Concerted efforts are needed to maximize the coverage in underperforming provinces and districts by increasing focus on supervision, as well as monitoring and evaluation. To target lagging areas, district-specific strategies can be developed that are better aligned with local norms and culture. Performance-based incentives for outreach workers and vaccinators could be explored to improve vaccine uptake. For hard-to-reach areas, mobile teams can be created. · Further strengthen service delivery systems: The number of vaccination sites may need to be increased to reduce waiting time, particularly in densely populated areas. The capacity of the districtbased EPI teams needs to be strengthened to improve service delivery. Training and use of information technology can enhance the capacity of the vaccinators and EPI teams. · Ensuring sustainable financing for immunization: One of the key achievements under NISP is the transfer of immunization-related budget from development partner financing to the government's share. In addition, dedicated accounting codes have been created to track immunization-related budget and expenditure. This helps the government in projecting total requirements and estimating needs. Given the changing burden of diseases, it is critical to ensure that the budgetary needs for immunization are adequately financed and the program is not hampered due to financial constraints. · Foster partnership: Partnership with the private sector can improve the efficacy of the EPI program. It can also help in promoting vaccine uptake and reaching out to areas where there are limited vaccination sites. There are good practices documented by other countries in the region that Pakistan can explore. · Explore innovative approaches for increasing demand for vaccination: conditional or coresponsibility cash transfers (CCT) have proven to be effective in improving HNP outcomes, particularly creating demand for immunization, in many countries in Asia and Africa. Under a CCT, cash is given to targeted beneficiaries upon fulfillment of conditions or coresponsibilities designed to promote uptake of selected services, such as vaccination. This is being piloted under NISP and can be rolled out if proven successful in the context of Pakistan. 4. Conclusions and way forward 28 16
